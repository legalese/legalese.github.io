OpenAI released ChatGPT on 30 November 2022, and it is genuinely remarkable. A million people signed up in the first week. For legal tasks involving language — drafting contracts, summarising judgments, explaining concepts in plain English — it is a step change. Legal work that took hours now takes minutes. That is not hype; it is what we are seeing.

The limitation is specific and important. For tasks requiring determinate answers — is this compliant? does this claimant qualify? — ChatGPT has a structural constraint: it predicts plausible text rather than executing rules. It cannot know when it is wrong. This is not a bug that larger models will fix — it is a property of the probabilistic approach. A language model has no internal check against legal reality.

For determinate legal reasoning, the solution is to pair LLMs with a rules engine. The LLM handles language brilliantly; the rules engine executes the applicable rules and produces an auditable answer. ChatGPT is a milestone for legal language. The compiler for legal reasoning is still being built — and that is what L4 is.
