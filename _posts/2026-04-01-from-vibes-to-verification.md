## Code Has Compilers. Law Has… Opinions.

Here's something that gets lost in the hype: AI didn't get good at coding by magic. It got good because code has a **deterministic evaluator**. You write a function, the compiler tells you whether it's valid, you run it and see if the output is correct. Right or wrong. No ambiguity.

That tight feedback loop — write, compile, run, learn — is what allowed large language models to go from generating plausible-looking code to generating *working* code. The model could try millions of approaches and get objective, instant feedback on every single one.

Now ask yourself: what's the equivalent feedback loop for law?

There isn't one. When AI generates a legal interpretation, a contract clause, or a compliance check, there's no compiler. No runtime. No test suite. There's just… a vibe. It sounds right. It *feels* authoritative. But there's nothing to run it against.

That's not an AI problem. That's an infrastructure problem.

## The Hallucination Problem Everyone Is Aware of but Quietly Ignoring

We all know AI hallucinates. The famous cases of [lawyers citing fake cases](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-65735769) generated by ChatGPT made headlines. But those were the obvious failures — the ones that got caught because someone checked.

What about the subtle ones? The clause that's *almost* right but creates an unintended loophole. The compliance check that *sounds* correct but misses an edge case in the regulation. The policy interpretation that's 95% accurate but wrong on the 5% that matters.

In software, we solved this decades ago. We don't trust that code "looks right." We run it. We test it. We have type checkers, linters, test suites, and CI pipelines that catch errors before they reach production.

Legal language has none of this. And rather than building the infrastructure to verify AI's legal output, we're doing something remarkable: we're shipping it anyway and hoping for the best.

Every enterprise deploying AI for contract review, compliance checking, or regulatory analysis is essentially running untested code in production. They know it. They're quietly ignoring it. The regulators know it too, which is why AI governance frameworks are proliferating — but those frameworks are themselves written in ambiguous natural language, which brings us right back to square one.

## Rules-as-Code: The Compiler for Law

This is the core idea behind what we're building at Legalese: **give rules a runtime.**

Our language, [L4](https://legalese.com/l4), lets you express legislation, regulations, policies, contract terms — any kind of rule — as executable code. But not code that looks like Java or Python. Code that reads like the legal text it represents:

```l4
GIVEN person IS A Person
DECIDE `the person is eligible for benefits` IF
    person `is a citizen`
    AND person `has resided for at least 5 years`
    AND NOT person `has been disqualified`
```

That's not pseudocode. That's real L4 that compiles, type-checks, and runs. A lawyer can read it. A developer can integrate it. And critically: **an AI can test itself against it.**

Once a rule exists in L4, it becomes a deterministic evaluator — exactly the kind of feedback mechanism that made AI good at coding. Instead of asking "does this contract comply with the regulation?" and getting a probabilistic answer, you can *run* the regulation against the contract and get a definitive yes or no, with a full evaluation trace showing exactly which conditions were met or failed.

This isn't about replacing lawyers or AI. It's about giving both of them something neither has today: **ground truth for rules.**

## The Feedback Loop That Changes Everything

Here's why this matters beyond just "better legal tech."

AI got good at coding because of a flywheel: AI writes code → compiler/runtime gives deterministic feedback → AI learns → AI writes better code. This loop ran billions of times across millions of programs.

We're building the same flywheel for rules:

1. **AI helps codify rules** in L4 — using the same coding ability it already has
2. **Codified rules run deterministically** — giving AI (and humans) ground truth
3. **AI tests itself against those rules** — the same feedback loop that made it good at coding
4. **More reliable AI helps codify more rules** — and the cycle accelerates

This is not a hypothetical. It's already happening. When an AI agent calls an L4 rule via MCP, it's not guessing at compliance — it's *executing* compliance. When a developer uses AI to draft L4 and then runs the type checker, they're getting the same deterministic feedback that makes AI-assisted coding so productive.

The implication for enterprises is profound: instead of deploying AI with fingers crossed, hoping it interprets your policies correctly, you **deploy the policies themselves as executable services** and let any AI — or any system — call them directly.

Same rules. Every time. Deterministically. Auditably.

## My Cofounder Called It in 2017

In January 2017, my cofounder Meng Weng Wong published a three-part series on [the problem with legal](/posts/the-problem-with-legal), [70 years of legal informatics](/posts/prior-art-legal-informatics), and the case for [computational law](/posts/computational-law). It's worth reading in full, but here's the short version:

**The legal system has a quality problem.** Not a minor one. Meng catalogued a parade of comma-related disasters — an [Oxford comma dispute](https://qz.com/932004/the-oxford-comma-a-maine-court-settled-the-grammar-debate-over-serial-commas-with-a-ruling-on-overtime-pay-for-dairy-truck-drivers/) worth millions, a [misplaced comma in 1872](https://priceonomics.com/the-most-expensive-typo-in-legislative-history/) that cost taxpayers $38 million in today's dollars, commas that [decided gun rights](http://www.businessinsider.com/the-comma-in-the-second-amendment-2013-8). He described contract drafting as "pair programming by correspondence," the way chess used to be played during the Cold War. Slow, error-prone, and impossibly quaint.

**The academic world saw this coming.** Seventy years of research into legal informatics — from [Loevinger's Jurimetrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurimetrics) in 1948 to the [British Nationality Act in Prolog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Nationality_Act_Implementation_Using_Prolog) in the 1980s — had produced mountains of theory about formalizing law. Almost none of it had made it into practice. As Malcolm Gladwell put it: *"That brilliant learning sometimes gets trapped in academia and never sees the light of day."*

**The fix isn't better Word templates.** Meng argued that law needed what every other complex domain eventually gets: a domain-specific programming language. Not incremental automation, but a fundamental shift in how rules are expressed — from ambiguous prose to executable, testable, verifiable code.

He was right. But the timing was early. In 2017, the idea of non-programmers writing formal logic was a tough sell. Then AI changed everything — and now we're building what he envisioned.

What we have today is already useful. What's coming next is transformative.

**Formal verification** will find contradictions and gaps in legislation automatically — the kind of analysis that currently takes legal teams weeks of manual review.

**Natural language generation** will round-trip L4 back to polished English (or any language), so rules can be maintained in L4 and published as traditional legal text — provably consistent across translations.

Nine years ago, Meng wrote that contracts have *"more in common with processor design than a web page"* — that they needed the rigour of formal methods, not the "move fast and break things" ethos of web development. He argued that *"software is eating the world"* but hadn't yet eaten law.

Now AI is eating software. The question isn't whether AI will transform how rules are written, interpreted, and enforced. It will. The question is whether that transformation will be built on vibes or on verification.
